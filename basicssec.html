Nine steps from a humble multiplication to a machine that writes

Strip away the jargon and modern generative AI is a short chain of ideas you already have the maths for: lists of numbers, multiply-and-add, a squashing function, and an error you shrink step by step. This page builds the chain one link at a time. Every stage is interactive, and every stage ends with a live spreadsheet you can edit and Python that really runs, right here in the page.

vectors → matrices → neurons → layers → learning → meaning → attention → transformers → generation

1Vectors 2Matrix × 3Neuron 4Layers 5Learning 6Meaning 7Attention 8Transformer 9Generation

How to use this series

Work top to bottom; each stage uses only the stages before it. Play with the widget until the idea feels obvious, then open the green Excel panel and the blue Python panel. These are not instructions to read: the green panel is a working mini-spreadsheet (click any shaded cell to see its formula, edit any number, everything recalculates), and the blue panel is real Python that runs in your browser when you press ▶ Run. Change the code. Break it. Fix it. That is the point.

By Stage 9 you will have every ingredient of a real large language model, not a cartoon of one. Real systems differ from this page in only two boring ways: more numbers (billions of weights instead of a handful) and more repetitions (dozens of identical layers instead of one).

Stage 1 · Data

Everything the machine knows is a vector

A vector is just an ordered list of numbers, like [3, 2]. Its superpower: one list can describe anything: a point, a pixel patch, a word, a song. And one tiny operation compares two of them: the dot product. Multiply matching entries, add the results. Big dot product ⇒ the vectors point the same way ⇒ the things they describe are similar. This single operation is the heartbeat of every stage below.

  Stage 3 · The unit
  
The neuron: weight, add, squash

  
Take a dot product, add a nudge called the bias, then pass the result through a simple activation function that decides how much signal flows on. That three-step unit is a neuron, and it's the only "brain cell" AI has. The most popular activation, ReLU, could not be simpler: negative in, zero out; positive in, unchanged out. That tiny kink is what lets stacked layers bend straight lines into any shape.

  Stage 4 · Depth
  
Layers: neurons stacked into a network

  
One neuron draws one straight boundary: useful, but weak. Put neurons side by side (a layer) and feed each layer's outputs into the next, and the network can bend and combine those boundaries into any pattern at all. Data flowing left-to-right through the stack is called the forward pass, and each layer is just Stage 2 + Stage 3: multiply by a weight matrix, add biases, apply ReLU.   Stage 5 · Training
  
Learning: roll downhill on the error

  
Where do the weights come from? Nobody writes them. The network starts random, measures how wrong it is with a single number (the loss), then nudges every weight in whichever direction shrinks that loss. The slope that tells it which way is downhill is the gradient; repeatedly stepping down the slope is gradient descent. The step size (learning rate) is the only knob: too small crawls, too big explodes. Feel both below.

  Stage 6 · Meaning
  
Embeddings: where meaning becomes geometry

  
To feed words into all that matrix machinery, each word is assigned its own learned vector: an embedding. Training pushes words that appear in similar places towards similar vectors, so meaning turns into distance: near = related. We measure "near" with cosine similarity, the dot product (Stage 1 again!) scaled to ignore length: +1 same direction, 0 unrelated, −1 opposite. Pick any two words on the map.

  Stage 7 · Context
  
Attention: every word looks at every other word

  
Embeddings give each word a meaning, but meaning depends on context. In "the crane ate a fish", crane is a bird; next to construction site it's a machine. Attention fixes this: each word scores its relevance to every other word (dot products of their vectors), the scores are squashed into percentages by softmax, and each word rebuilds itself as a weighted mix of the words it attends to.   Stage 8 · Assembly
  
The transformer: your seven ideas, bolted together

  
There is no new mathematics in this stage. That's the punchline. A transformer block is attention (Stage 7) followed by a small two-layer network (Stage 4), with the input added back on so information never gets lost. A large language model is: embeddings in → the same block repeated dozens of times → one final layer that scores every word in the vocabulary. All of it trained end-to-end by gradient descent (Stage 5). Step through the journey of one prompt:

  Stage 9 · Creation
  
Generation: pick a word, repeat forever

  
The transformer ends with a score for every word it knows. Softmax turns scores into probabilities; the model samples one word, appends it, and runs again, one word per lap, until the reply is done. One dial shapes its personality: temperature divides the scores before softmax. Low temperature → the favourite dominates (safe, repetitive). High → probabilities flatten (creative, risky). This is the actual dial behind every chatbot's "creativity" setting.

That's the whole machine.

  
Nine stages, no hand-waving: every modern generative model (chatbots, image makers, music models) is built from the chain you just walked. Scale it up (billions of weights, oceans of data, weeks of gradient descent) and it writes essays. The mathematics stays exactly this.

  
vectors → dot products → matrices → neurons → layers →
loss ↘ gradients ↘ learning → embeddings → attention → transformer → sample → repeat

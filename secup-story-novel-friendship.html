TRACK 01

Ten Years of Tape

spectrograms · embeddings · consent

The ward smelled of antiseptic and canteen Milo. Ren had been coming every day for six weeks, and every day he brought the same two things: homework Farhan ignored, and headphones Farhan didn't.

The tracheostomy tube sat below Farhan's collarbone like a full stop someone had punched into the middle of a sentence. The doctors were careful with their words, and Ren had learned to be careful with them too. Farhan was not "mute": the surgeons had spared what they could, and with a speaking valve he could push air past his vocal folds and manage a whisper. But a whisper cost him. Minutes a day, then a raw, burning ache, then notes typed on a cracked phone screen.

Farhan (typing): The group chat's dead. Eleven people watched my message and nobody replied.

Ren: They don't know what to say. That's not the same as not caring. But yeah. It's rubbish.

Farhan (typing): You know what's worse than losing your voice? Watching everyone act like YOU'RE the awkward silence.

Ren: Then let's be loud. I've been thinking. You didn't lose your voice, bro. The world lost its decoder. Those are different engineering problems.

Here was Ren's argument, sketched on the back of a physiotherapy schedule. Since Primary Three they had recorded everything: every voice memo, every hook Farhan freestyled on the 154 bus, every argument about whether a snare was too loud, all of it captured over Ren's beats and synced to a shared drive with the world's worst folder names. Ten years. Roughly 40 hours of Farhan talking, rapping, laughing, and telling Ren his mixes were muddy.

That wasn't a nostalgia archive. That was a dataset.

What a voice actually is

To build anything, they first had to answer a question that sounds philosophical but is brutally practical: what makes a voice a voice? A microphone only records air pressure wiggling thousands of times per second: a long list of numbers. Useless to stare at. So speech systems convert audio into a spectrogram: a picture where time runs left to right, pitch runs bottom to top, and brightness shows how much energy sits at each frequency. Farhan's growled "oi" and his sarcastic drawl each paint a different shape.

And once sound is a picture, the same trick that powers computer vision applies: a convolutional neural network reads the spectrogram exactly the way it reads a photo: as a grid of pixels, scanning small patches for edges and textures, then combining patches into bigger patterns. The network compresses each snippet of Farhan into an embedding: a vector of a few hundred numbers that captures how he sounds (the nasal edge on his vowels, the way his pitch dips before a punchline) while throwing away what he happened to be saying.

Two clips of Farhan land close together in that vector space. A clip of Ren lands far away. "Close" has a precise meaning: cosine similarity, the angle between two vectors: 1.0 means pointing the same way, 0 means unrelated.

voiceprint.py · is this clip Farhan?
# A voice embedding turns "how someone sounds" into a vector.
# Cosine similarity measures the angle between two voices.
import numpy as np

def cosine(a, b):
    return np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

# Toy 4-D embeddings (real ones have ~256 dimensions)
farhan_2019 = np.array([0.82, 0.31, -0.44, 0.15])  # bus freestyle
farhan_2025 = np.array([0.79, 0.35, -0.40, 0.11])  # ward whisper
ren_hyping  = np.array([-0.20, 0.88, 0.35, -0.10]) # wrong guy

print("Farhan vs Farhan:", round(cosine(farhan_2019, farhan_2025), 3))  # 0.998
print("Farhan vs Ren:   ", round(cosine(farhan_2019, ren_hyping), 3))   # -0.062
# Six years apart, one tracheostomy later: still the same direction.

When Ren ran a real version of this on the archive, the result made both of them go quiet. Farhan's whisper embeddings from the ward sat in the same neighbourhood as his 2019 freestyles. Weaker signal, same direction. The voice wasn't gone. It was quiet, not erased.

Whose keys?

Before a single training run, Ren stopped and did the unglamorous thing properly. Forty hours of someone's voice is not "content". It is the raw material for a system that can speak as that person. So they wrote it down like engineers: the recordings and every model trained on them belong to Farhan. Farhan holds the encryption keys. Farhan can revoke, delete, or veto any use, including Ren's. Every file got a provenance note: where it was recorded, when, and the consent status.

Ren: I know it feels dumb, signing a contract with your best friend.

Farhan (typing): It's not dumb. It's the first time since the surgery someone's asked my permission for anything. The nurses ask my mum.

Checkpoint 01 · Who holds the keys?

Ren and Farhan are drafting the data agreement for the voice archive. Which design puts consent where it belongs?

Provenance and revocable consent aren't paperwork. They are the load-bearing wall of everything the boys build next, and the reason Track 04 doesn't end their friendship.

Track 01 · Liner notes

  • Sound becomes image: a spectrogram lets a CNN read audio as a pixel grid, just like a photo.
  • Embeddings capture identity: a vector encodes how a voice sounds; cosine similarity measures how close two voices are.
  • A voice is biometric data: the person the voice belongs to holds the keys; consent must be documented, provenanced and revocable.
TRACK 02

Fine-tune, Don't Imitate

pretraining · fine-tuning · inference · preference as reward

Farhan came home in March. The hospital bed was replaced by his own bed, the beeping by the neighbour's renovation drill, and the visits became a standing appointment: every Saturday, ten to six, Ren's room, no exceptions. Ren's mum started cooking for two without being asked.

Now came the hard question. How do two sixteen-year-olds with a gaming laptop build a speech system? They don't, not from scratch. Training a speech model from zero takes hundreds of thousands of hours of audio and more computing power than their whole school owns. But they didn't need to start from zero, because the field doesn't work that way anymore.

Three stages, three jobs

Modern speech systems are built in stages, and mixing them up is how people end up either broke or disappointed:

Pretraining happens once, at enormous cost, usually inside a big lab: a general model learns from vast, diverse audio what human speech is: phonemes, rhythm, the physics of vocal tracts, a thousand accents. The boys downloaded an open-source pretrained model. It knew speech. It didn't know Farhan.

Fine-tuning is where their forty hours came in: continue training the pretrained model on a small, specific dataset so it adapts. Nights and weekends on Ren's laptop (and one guilty week of a rented GPU paid with hongbao money), the general model's parameters shifted towards one particular voice: the vowels, the timing, the almost-laugh Farhan puts under a line when he knows it's good.

Inference is using the trained model: Farhan types or whispers, Amp speaks. No learning happens at inference: the weights are frozen. It's the concert, not the rehearsal.

PRETRAIN  → learn speech in general  (a big lab, once, enormous data)
FINE-TUNE → adapt to Farhan        (the boys, weeks, 40 hours of tape)
INFERENCE → speak, right now      (Farhan's phone, daily, weights frozen)

His taste is the reward signal

The first fine-tuned Amp sounded like Farhan the way a wax statue looks like a person. Accurate and horrible. The vowels were right; the choices were wrong. It stressed syllables Farhan would never stress. It was polite. Farhan has never once been polite on a beat.

So they stole an idea from how chatbots are aligned: reinforcement learning from human feedback, scaled down to an audience of one. Every Saturday, Amp generated pairs of readings of the same line. Farhan scored them: that's me / that's a stranger. Those preferences trained a small reward model (a learned function that predicts which output Farhan would pick), and Amp was tuned to score higher on it. Not "sound good". Not "sound natural". Sound like the person whose taste is the reward.

Farhan (typing): Version 12 said "let's GO" like a bank advertisement.

Ren: Noted. Thumbs-down logged. The reward model has learned to fear you.

Farhan (typing): Good. My whole personality is a loss function now.

Saturday, week 19 · Your call

Farhan arrives grey-faced: a bad night, throat raw, zero whisper budget. The GPU is rented until midnight and the preference queue is full. Ren looks at him. What does Ren do?

There is no wrong door here. Both answers share the load-bearing rule: Farhan is the producer of his own voice, never the project. Amp adapts to him, never the reverse.

Why Amp lives on a phone

The final engineering fight of the season was about where inference runs. The easy route: a big model on a cloud server. But Farhan needs Amp in the canteen, in the MRT tunnel with no signal, in class, and he flatly refused to stream his voice, his words and his medical situation to someone else's computer every time he wanted to say "oi, wait for me lah".

So Ren distilled Amp: compressed the fine-tuned model into a small one that runs on a mid-range phone. It costs a fraction of the energy per sentence, works with zero signal, and the audio never leaves Farhan's pocket. A smaller model meant slightly less perfect prosody, a trade-off they took with open eyes, because privacy and availability were the product. Training is the expensive, rare thing. Inference is the daily thing. You optimise the daily thing for the life it has to live in.

Checkpoint 02 · Sort the stages

Four claims about Amp's life cycle. Exactly one is right; the others each smuggle one stage into another's job.

Bonus link: Farhan's thumbs-up/thumbs-down rounds are RLHF in miniature: human preference distilled into a reward signal. His taste is literally the objective function.

Track 02 · Liner notes

  • Pretrain → fine-tune → inference: learn speech in general, adapt to one person, then run with frozen weights.
  • RLHF in miniature: "that's me / that's a stranger" preferences train a reward model; alignment means aligned to Farhan.
  • Deploy for the life it lives in: a distilled on-device model buys privacy, offline use and low energy at the price of a little polish.
TRACK 03

Honest Evaluation

word-error rate · precision/recall · overfitting

By June, Amp had two halves. The ear: a recogniser that turned Farhan's limited whisper into text, so he could speak a few words and have Amp expand them aloud. The mouth: the synthesiser from Track 02. And Ren, drunk on progress, declared version 23 "basically done".

Farhan typed one line: "Prove it."

Counting what goes wrong

The industry-standard score for the ear is word-error rate (WER): compare the transcript against what was really said, count every substitution (wrong word), deletion (missed word) and insertion (invented word), and divide by the number of words in the true sentence.

wer.py · the ear's report card
# Word-error rate: (S + D + I) / N
# S = substitutions, D = deletions, I = insertions,
# N = words in the REFERENCE (what Farhan actually whispered)

def wer(subs, dels, ins, n_ref):
    return (subs + dels + ins) / n_ref

# Saturday test set, version 23:
print("Good day :", wer(subs=1, dels=0, ins=0, n_ref=25))  # 0.04 → 4%
print("Bad day  :", wer(subs=2, dels=1, ins=1, n_ref=20))  # 0.20 → 20%
# One number per day is not one number per system.
# A model that only works on good days doesn't work.

That split, good days versus bad days, was Farhan's insistence, and it changed everything. Post-tracheostomy voices are not constant. On a rested morning with the valve freshly cleaned, his whisper carried; after physiotherapy, or on a low day, it frayed to almost nothing. An average over all days would have hidden the exact failures that hurt most. So every evaluation ran twice, and the bad-day number was the one that counted. You evaluate a system on the person's hardest hour, because that's when they need it.

Checkpoint 03 · Score the bad day

Bad-day test: Farhan whispers a reference sentence of 20 words. Amp's transcript gets 2 words wrong (substitutions), misses 1 word entirely (deletion) and invents 1 word that was never said (insertion). What is the word-error rate?

Same errors, different denominators, wildly different stories. Fixing the denominator is half of honest evaluation.

The metric that missed the point

Then came the humbling part. Version 25 hit 6% bad-day WER, and Farhan hated it. The words were right. The meaning was wrong. Farhan is famous across two schools for sarcasm you could bottle, and Amp read his most cutting lines with the warm sincerity of a station announcement. WER counts words; it is stone deaf to intent.

So they built a second evaluation from Farhan's own rubric, a checklist he wrote himself: Did it keep my timing? Did the sarcasm survive? Would my mum know it wasn't me? Each Saturday, a blind test: twenty lines, half real archive audio, half Amp, scored on the rubric by Farhan, Ren, and Ren's mum (a devastating judge). The lesson under the lesson: a metric is a claim about what matters, and off-the-shelf metrics encode someone else's claim. When precision on "did the flagged sarcastic lines actually land as sarcastic" mattered, they counted true and false positives like any classifier: of 20 lines Amp marked sarcastic, 18 genuinely were (precision 0.90), but it found only 18 of the 24 sarcastic lines in the set (recall 0.75). Amp was cautious with the attitude. Farhan wanted the attitude.

The overfitting week

Week 31 nearly broke morale. Ren, chasing the rubric scores, fine-tuned hard on their cleanest data: studio-quality archive tape, healthy-voice Farhan, treated room. The metrics on the training-style data soared. Then Farhan tried Amp in the polyclinic waiting room after a check-up (tired voice, air-con roar) and it fell apart. 31% WER. Amp had become a specialist in Studio Farhan, a person who no longer entirely existed, and had forgotten hospital Farhan, the person it was for.

Classic overfitting: the model memorised the quirks of its training data instead of learning the pattern underneath. The fix was as unglamorous as the diagnosis was dramatic: more varied data, held-out tests, earlier stopping. They recorded new samples on bad days on purpose (short sessions, Farhan's call, always). They augmented old tape with realistic noise: bus rumble, canteen clatter. And they locked away a test set Amp never trained on, so the score they trusted was earned on data the model had never seen.

Farhan (typing): So the model fell in love with a version of me from 2021.

Ren: Yeah. My fault. I optimised for the Farhan who was easy to measure.

Farhan (typing): Don't do that. Everyone does that. Build for the Tuesday-afternoon me.

Track 03 · Liner notes

  • WER = (S+D+I)/N: count substitutions, deletions and insertions against the reference, and report the bad-day number, not the flattering average.
  • Metrics are claims: WER can be excellent while the sarcasm dies. Precision/recall on what the user actually cares about beats a borrowed benchmark.
  • Overfitting wears a good report card: brilliant on training-like data, useless in the polyclinic. Held-out test sets and varied data keep you honest.
TRACK 04

The Betrayal That Almost Was

voice cloning · temperature · provenance · watermarking

It surfaced on a Thursday, the way these things do: forwarded, then forwarded again, then everywhere. Ninety seconds of audio titled "FARHAN COMES FOR MERIDIAN SEC (LEAKED DISS)". Farhan's voice, unmistakably, sickeningly his, sneering insults at half their friends, at a rival crew, at a teacher who had visited him in hospital.

Except Farhan hadn't recorded ninety seconds of anything in over a year. He physically couldn't.

The trail was short. A classmate had ripped a thirty-second demo clip from an old shared playlist, fed it to a free online voice-cloning site, typed a script, and pressed generate. Twenty minutes of effort. He thought it was funny.

Farhan (typing, hands shaking): Delete Amp. Delete the archive. All of it.

Ren: Farhan…

Farhan (typing): I spent a year learning to be okay with a machine speaking for me because it was MINE. Now my voice belongs to everyone but me. Some kid with a browser owns it as much as I do.

Ren didn't argue that night. This part matters: he didn't defend the technology, didn't say but we built it safely, didn't treat a year of engineering as more important than his friend shaking on the void deck. He said: "If you want it gone, it's gone. Your keys. But give me one week to fight first." Farhan gave him the week.

Know your enemy's pipeline

Their counter-attack started with understanding exactly how the fake was made, because the cloner and Amp are, uncomfortably, cousins. A modern voice synthesiser works like the text models they'd studied: the script is broken into tokens, mapped to embeddings, passed through attention layers that decide how each sound should lean on its neighbours, and the model predicts the audio piece by piece as a sequence of probabilities, then samples from those probabilities to produce sound. A short reference clip conditions the whole pipeline to steer the output towards one speaker's timbre. Thirty seconds is genuinely enough for a passable clone. Everyone should know that; almost nobody does.

And that word samples hides a dial: temperature. Low temperature makes the model take the safest, most probable option at every step: smooth, controlled, eerily consistent. Higher temperature admits more randomness: livelier, riskier, more human-messy. The diss track had been generated at the site's cautious default, and once you knew to listen for it, you couldn't unhear it.

REAL FARHAN, 90 seconds of archive tape: 14 audible breaths, 2 stumbles he ran with,
pitch drifting flat when he gets heated, one cough, timing that pushes and drags the beat.

THE FAKE, 90 seconds: zero breaths. Zero stumbles. Every line the same loudness,
the same pace, the same perfect placement. No bad takes, because there were no takes.
Checkpoint 04 · Spot the clone

Ren plays both clips to the class. Which acoustic tell most reliably exposed this fake?

Honest caveat, which Ren gave the assembly too: better forgers raise the temperature and splice in fake breaths. Tells help today; they are not a permanent defence. That is why the real fixes are structural.

Engineers and friends

The week produced three structural fixes, and the order mattered. Friend things first, engineer things after:

1 · The licence. They formalised Track 01's handshake into a signed voice licence: Farhan's voice may be synthesised only by Amp, only for Farhan's own communication, only while he consents, revocable in writing at any time, by him alone. Not because paper stops a forger, but because it draws the public line the school, both families, and any future dispute can stand on. Accountability needs a document.

2 · The watermark. Every clip Amp produces now carries a signature: an inaudible pattern woven through the audio plus a signed provenance tag in the file: generated by Amp, for Farhan, at this timestamp. Anything claiming to be Farhan's synthetic voice without the mark is counterfeit by definition. They flipped the burden of proof.

3 · The assembly. The part nobody expected: instead of hiding, they went on stage. Ren cloned his own voice live in front of the whole school (thirty seconds of reference, a typed script, press generate) and let a hall full of students hear how trivially "audio evidence" can be manufactured, and how the too-smooth tell gives cheap fakes away. The classmate got his consequences from the discipline master; the school got something rarer: 800 students who will never again believe a voice clip just because it sounds like someone. Nobody gets fooled twice on Ren's watch.

Farhan (typing): You could have just reported him and moved on.

Ren: Reporting him fixes Tuesday. Teaching eight hundred people fixes the school. And you standing next to me up there fixes the story: you're not the kid who got faked. You're the guy who showed everyone how it works.

On Sunday, Farhan sent Ren a single line: "Keep the archive. Keys stay with me." The friendship had been the point all along; the technology just had to be rebuilt until it agreed.

Track 04 · Liner notes

  • Synthesis is a pipeline: tokens → embeddings → attention → probabilities → sampling; a 30-second clip can condition it to clone a timbre.
  • Temperature leaves fingerprints: safe, low-temperature sampling sounds too smooth: no breaths, no stumbles, no drift. Useful tell; not a permanent one.
  • Structural defences beat detection: watermarking and signed provenance on all legitimate output, plus a revocable licence, flip the burden of proof, and teaching the community closes the loop.
TRACK 05

Open Mic

a live agent pipeline · human override · "does it feel like us?"

The community club open mic. A borrowed PA, plastic chairs, an auntie selling drinks at the back. Fourteen months after the ward, Farhan and Ren's names were on the list, third from last: AMP · R. Kwan & F. Iskandar.

The system on stage

Live performance is where Amp stopped being a model and became an agent: a system running a loop, not answering a single prompt. Ren drew the loop on the back of the setlist:

THE STAGE LOOP (every bar, ~1.8 seconds):
1 LISTEN  · track Ren's beat + Farhan's taps and whisper fragments
2 PREDICT · from the lyric sheet + tempo, plan the next phrase's timing and stress
3 CHECK   · does the plan match Farhan's live conducting? (pause / punch / stretch)
4 SPEAK   · synthesise the phrase, watermarked, in his voice
5 OBSERVE · did it land where Farhan wanted? adjust, go to 1

HARD RULE, wired above everything: FARHAN'S OVERRIDE ALWAYS WINS.
His kill-switch mutes Amp mid-syllable. No confidence score outranks it.

Step 3 is the soul of the design. Amp doesn't just read lyrics aloud; Farhan conducts it in real time from a controller taped to the mic stand: hold for a pause, double-tap to punch a word, slide to stretch a phrase. The model predicts; the human disposes. And the override rule isn't politeness, it's architecture: the mute command is wired upstream of the synthesiser, so there is no state Amp can be in (mid-word, mid-flourish, ninety-nine-percent confident) where Farhan cannot silence it instantly. They tested it two hundred times. It is the one feature with a required pass rate of 100%.

Checkpoint 05 · Why must the override always win?

A well-meaning uncle at the soundcheck suggests: "If Amp is 99% confident in its phrasing, maybe it should finish the line even if Farhan taps stop; he might have tapped by accident." Why do the boys refuse?

Generalise it: in any agent that acts on a person's behalf (speaking, sending, buying, driving), the human override must sit above the model's confidence, not beside it. That is what "human in the loop by design" means.

The metric that matters

How do you evaluate a system like this? Not WER: nobody in a crowd counts substitutions. Months before, the boys had written their governing metric on a sticky note above Ren's desk, and every design decision since had answered to it: does it feel like us? Concretely: after each rehearsal, both of them scored the set: did the phrasing sound like Farhan's intent, did Ren's drops land with Amp's lines, did anything feel like a machine performing at people instead of two friends performing for them. Version by version they logged the scores, found the weakest moment, fixed exactly that, and re-ran. Evaluate, surface the limitation, iterate. The last logged limitation, in Farhan's handwriting: "Amp still can't do the thing where I almost laugh. Keep that one. Some things should stay mine."

Ten minutes to stage · Your call

The emcee asks how to introduce them. Ren looks at Farhan. Farhan looks at the ceiling, then types two options and turns the phone around.

Both doors lead to the same non-negotiable: the audience learns the truth from Farhan and Ren, on their terms, before the night ends. Synthetic media with the consent, provenance and honesty handled: that was always the act.

Four bars

The set was eight minutes. Ren on the decks, head down, hands sure. Farhan centre stage, conducting his own voice: pausing it, punching it, stretching a line until the room grinned. Amp sounded like him because it had been graded by him, ten thousand thumb-votes deep. The crowd stopped noticing the laptop by the second verse. That was the whole idea.

Then, before the last four bars, Farhan tapped the kill-switch himself.

Silence. Ren pulled the beat down to almost nothing. He knew; of course he knew; it was the only part they'd never rehearsed with Amp at all. Farhan lifted the mic. And in a whisper that cost him, four bars, barely louder than breath… the plastic chairs creaked as the whole room leaned in.

Amp could hit every phoneme he couldn't. But eighty people holding their breath to catch a real voice at the edge of what it could do: no synthesiser gets that. The triumph wasn't that the machine spoke well. It was that the machine knew when to shut up, because the person it belonged to told it to, and the friend at the decks had built it that way from the first Saturday.

Farhan (typing, on the bus home): You knew I was going to take the last four bars.

Ren: Since week one, bro. Why do you think the kill-switch got the 100% test suite?

The 154 bus, upper deck, front row. One pair of earphones split between them, left bud, right bud, like they were ten years old and the shared drive was still empty. The recording of tonight already syncing to it: hour forty-one of the archive, keys held where they belong.

Amp could say almost anything in Farhan's voice. It never had to say what mattered: Ren already knew.

Track 05 · Liner notes

  • An agent is a loop: listen → predict → check → act → observe, re-planning every cycle against the live world.
  • Override above confidence: when a system acts as a person, that person's stop signal must be architecturally unbeatable; consent is real-time, not a settings page.
  • Evaluate against your own metric: "does it feel like us?": score it, find the weakest moment, iterate, and know which limitations to fix and which to keep.

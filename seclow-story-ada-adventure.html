CHAPTER ONE

Codes & Numbers

How words and pictures become numbers

The old gate at the edge of the valley had changed. When Ada was little it just asked riddles. Now a smooth voice spoke from a glowing panel: “I am a machine. I do not read letters. Give me numbers, and I will understand.” Ida tilted her head. Ada smiled; she had grown up too, and this year she wanted to know how the machine really worked.

Words become tokens, tokens become numbers

A computer never sees the word “strawberry” the way you do. First it chops text into tokens: small chunks, often word-pieces. Then it swaps each token for a number called an ID, because maths is all a model can do.

Ada wrote out a sentence and split it by hand:

PYTHON · a tiny tokeniser
# turn text into tokens, then into ID numbers
text   = "AI is fun"
tokens = text.lower().split()        # ['ai', 'is', 'fun']
vocab  = {"ai": 1, "is": 2, "fun": 3}   # each token has an ID
ids    = [vocab[t] for t in tokens]  # [1, 2, 3]
print(ids)

Real models use sub-word tokens, so a rare word like “unhappiness” might become un + happi + ness. That one detail explains a famous puzzle.

🔍 Glass-box moment Ask a chatbot “how many rs are in strawberry?” and it may get it wrong. Why? It never sees the letters; it sees tokens like [st, raw, berry], each stored as a single ID number. Counting letters means peering inside a chunk it treats as a single number. Once you know how the representation is built, the mistake stops being spooky and starts being obvious.

Meaning as coordinates

Numbers can do more than label words; they can place them. Each token is given a list of numbers called an embedding, like a coordinate on a map of meaning. Words that mean similar things sit close together; “cat” and “kitten” are neighbours, while “cat” and “helicopter” are far apart.

Because they are coordinates, you can even do arithmetic with meaning. A classic result:

✨ Try the analogy king − man + woman ≈ queen. Follow the arrows on the map of meaning: start at king, take away “man-ness”, add “woman-ness”, and you land near queen. Ada thought that was the most magical-yet-logical thing she had ever seen.

Pictures are numbers too

The gate also showed Ida a photo, as a grid of numbers. Every tiny square (a pixel) is a number for how bright it is, from 0 (black) to 255 (white). Stack three grids for red, green and blue, and you have a full-colour picture the machine can compute with. “So a smiley face,” Ada said, “is just a spreadsheet.” The gate beeped happily.

📓 What Ada learned

I know text is split into tokens then turned into ID numbers; words also get embeddings, and images are grids of pixel numbers.
I can hand-tokenise a sentence, explain a letter-miscount, and read an embedding space.
I care about reasoning from how a representation is built: the glass-box habit.

CHAPTER TWO

Learning Meadow

A neuron, the workflow, and your first classifier

In the meadow, a farmer robot was struggling to tell ripe berries from unripe ones. “I've written a hundred rules,” it sighed, “and I still get it wrong.” Ada knew the secret: some problems are too messy for fixed rules. You don't tell the machine the answer; you let it learn from examples.

Inside a single neuron

The smallest learner is a neuron. It takes some inputs, gives each one a weight (how much it matters), adds them up, and if the total is high enough, it “fires”. That's it: a weighted sum and a decision.

PYTHON · one neuron, by hand
def neuron(inputs, weights, bias):
    total = bias
    for x, w in zip(inputs, weights):
        total += x * w                  # the weighted sum
    return 1 if total >= 0 else 0       # 1 means it "fires"

# inputs = [spottiness, softness] of a berry
print(neuron([0.9, 0.7], [1.5, 1.0], -1.4))   # -> 1  (ripe!)

Change the weights and the neuron changes its mind. Learning is really just the machine adjusting its own weights until it gets more answers right. Stack thousands of these together and you have a neural network.

Three ways a machine can learn

Ada sketched three paths in her notebook:

🏷️ Supervised: learn from labelled examples (“this berry is ripe, that one isn't”).
🧩 Unsupervised: no labels; find groups by yourself (a method called k-means clusters similar things together).
🏆 Reinforcement: learn from rewards, by trial and error, like a robot mouse earning cheese.

The workflow, and why we hide the test data

Real machine learning follows a set order: data → train → test → evaluate → improve. The clever trick is to hold back some data the model never sees during training. If it only did well on questions it had already seen, that's memorising, not learning. The hidden test set is the real exam.

⚠️ Watch out Never test a model on the same data you trained it on. A model can score 100% by memorising, and still fail on anything new. Ada calls this “studying the answer key instead of the subject”.

To score it, we fill in a confusion matrix: a little grid of what the model said versus what was true:

said “ripe”said “unripe”
really ripe✔ TP✘ FN
really unripe✘ FP✔ TN

The green squares are correct (true positives and true negatives). The pink squares are mistakes: a false positive (called ripe but wasn't) and a false negative (missed a ripe one). Counting these tells you exactly how your model is wrong, not just that it is.

📓 What Ada learned

I know a neuron is a weighted sum that “fires”; machines learn supervised, unsupervised or by reward; we hold out test data and read a confusion matrix.
I can code a neuron and a simple classifier, run a train/test split, and read a confusion matrix.
I care about judging a model by evidence on unseen data, not memorised scores.

CHAPTER THREE

Making Workshop

Predicting the next token, attention and diffusion

The workshop smelled of paint and electricity. Here, machines didn't just sort things; they made new things: sentences, pictures, even music. “But how,” Ada wondered aloud, “does a computer write?” The answer turned out to be a guessing game played very, very fast.

One token at a time

A language model writes by predicting the next token. It looks at what came before and works out the probability of every possible next token, then picks a likely one, and repeats. Write, predict, write, predict. That's the whole trick.

Ada built the simplest version by hand: a bigram model, which just counts which word tends to follow which.

PYTHON · be the language model
from collections import defaultdict, Counter

text  = "i am sam . sam i am . i do not like ham"
words = text.split()

after = defaultdict(Counter)
for a, b in zip(words, words[1:]):     # count every word-pair
    after[a][b] += 1

print(after["i"].most_common())         # what usually follows "i"?
# -> [('am', 2), ('do', 1)], so "i" is most often followed by "am"

To generate text, start with a word and keep picking a likely next one. Real models (with billions of examples) do the same thing; they just look back much further and learn far richer patterns than simple counts.

Attention: looking back to choose well

How does a model know that in “the tired cat chased its tail, then it slept”, the word “it” means the cat? Through attention: when choosing the next token, the model weighs how relevant each earlier word is, and pays more “attention” to the ones that matter. It's like glancing back at the important words in a sentence before you finish it.

Diffusion: pictures out of noise

Image generators use a different, beautiful idea called diffusion. Imagine a photo slowly buried under TV static until it's pure noise. A diffusion model learns to run that backwards: starting from random static and removing noise step by step, nudged by your prompt, until a picture appears. Ada thought of it as a sculptor chipping a statue out of fog.

📓 What Ada learned

I know an LLM writes by predicting a likely next token; attention weighs earlier words; diffusion builds images by reversing noise.
I can build a bigram next-token predictor by hand and use it to generate text.
I care about explaining the mechanism, not calling it magic.

CHAPTER FOUR

Helper Harbor

Prompting well, and how an agent uses tools

At the harbor, boats came and went carrying messages to a great AI in the lighthouse. Ada noticed something: the sailors who got useful answers all asked in a certain way. The ones who shouted “HELP!” got useless replies. How you ask, it turned out, matters as much as what you ask.

A clear prompt

A good prompt gives the model a role, some context, a clear task, and, best of all, an example or two to copy. Frameworks like CLEAR remind you to be concise, give context, and show what “good” looks like. Compare these:

❌ “write about dogs”  →  vague, you'll get anything.
✅ “You are a vet. In 3 friendly sentences for a 10-year-old, explain why puppies chew things.”

The second gives a role (vet), an audience (10-year-old), a length (3 sentences) and a tone (friendly). Showing examples is called few-shot prompting:

PROMPT · few-shot examples
Turn each messy note into a polite request.

messy:  "send file now"
polite: "Could you please send the file when you get a chance?"

messy:  "fix my code"
polite: ___          # the model copies the pattern to fill this in

Ada learned to compare outputs: try two prompts, keep the one that works better. Prompting is a skill you improve by testing, just like everything else in AI.

From chatbot to agent

A plain chatbot only talks. An agent can act. It runs a loop (Sense → Think → Act) and, crucially, it can call tools: a calculator, a search, a calendar. The model decides when it needs help it can't give reliably on its own.

PSEUDOCODE · an agent loop with a tool
goal = "What is 17% of 240?"

loop:
    thought = model.think(goal)
    if thought.needs_a_tool:
        result = calculator("0.17 * 240")   # call a tool
        model.remember(result)              # 40.8
    else:
        answer = model.reply(goal)          # done: reply
        stop
🔍 When does an agent need a tool? For anything a language model can't do reliably from memory: exact arithmetic, today's date, live news, or looking up a fact. A wise agent knows its own limits and reaches for the right tool, or asks a human.

📓 What Ada learned

I know a clear prompt with examples steers a model; an agent loops Sense → Think → Act and calls tools.
I can use a prompt framework, compare outputs, and trace where an agent needs a tool.
I care about designing systems that ask a tool, or a human, when unsure.

CHAPTER FIVE

Guardian's Grove

Why AI can be confidently wrong, and staying safe

The last land was quiet and green. An old owl watched Ada from a branch. “You've learned how the machines work,” she said. “Now learn to use them wisely. The most dangerous AI is not the one that fails loudly; it's the one that is wrong while sounding perfectly sure.”

Confident, and wrong

Remember Chapter 3: a language model is trained to produce the most plausible next token, not the most true one. Usually plausible and true match. But sometimes the smoothest-sounding answer is simply made up. This is called a hallucination: a confident, fluent, wrong answer, like an invented book title or a fake “fact”.

⚠️ The trap Fluent does not mean correct. A model can write a beautiful paragraph citing a study that never existed. Confidence is not evidence.

Deepfakes and synthetic media

Generative AI can also fake faces, voices and videos that look real. These deepfakes can spread misinformation: a video of someone “saying” something they never said. Knowing this exists is the first defence: pause before you believe or share.

A routine you can trust

Ada wrote a checklist in the front of her notebook, so she'd never forget it:

A ROUTINE · before you trust an AI answer
1. Ask: could this be plausible but false?
2. Cross-check it against a trusted source.
3. Look for a real citation or evidence.
4. If it matters (health, money, safety): ask a human expert.
5. Name the risk: who could be harmed if it is wrong?

When she builds things, she adds safeguards: keep a human in the loop, show where answers came from (explainability), and test with all kinds of people so the system is fair for everyone (inclusive design).

📓 What Ada learned

I know models optimise for plausibility, not truth, so they can hallucinate; deepfakes enable misinformation.
I can apply a verification routine, flag risks, and propose safeguards.
I care about verifying before trusting, and designing for people's safety.

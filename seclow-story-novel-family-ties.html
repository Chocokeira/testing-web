CHAPTER ONE

Pages as Pixels

How a damaged page becomes something a computer can hold

The flood came from a split pipe under the sink, quietly, on a Tuesday night. By morning the bottom shelf of the kitchen dresser, the shelf with Po-Po's recipe books, had stood in four centimetres of water for eight hours. Mei-Ling, thirteen, found her grandmother at the kitchen table with the swollen books spread on tea towels, peeling pages apart with the gentleness of a surgeon.

Po-Po
"Sixty years in October. Sixty years since your great-grandfather opened this kitchen, and I wanted to cook the whole book for the family. Now look. The ink swims. Half the pages have let go of their spines. I don't even know which page belongs to which dish any more."

Mei
"I can't cook them back, Po-Po. But I can scan them. All of them. And if a computer can hold them, maybe we can sort them, read the faded bits, put them back in order. Together… you'd have to tell me what everything means."

Po-Po looked at her for a long moment, then slid the least-damaged book across the table. "Start with the soup pages," she said. "Soup forgives."

What the scanner actually sees

Mei's scanner doesn't see "a recipe". It sees a grid of numbers. Each tiny square of the page, each pixel, gets a brightness value, usually from 0 (black ink) to 255 (white paper). A greyscale scan of a page is just thousands of those numbers arranged in rows and columns. That's it. That's how a computer "sees": no paper, no ink, no memory of Sunday dinners… numbers in a grid.

Here is a tiny 3×3 patch from a water-stained corner. Paper should read near 255; ink near 0. Water damage drags everything into the murky middle:

90100110 95105115 85100100
🔍 Why smudges fool machines A character-reading model learned from examples where ink was dark (near 0) and paper was bright (near 255). A smudge pushes whole regions to middling values like 100, numbers the model rarely saw during training. To us it's "obviously a water stain over the character for salt". To the model it's a pattern of numbers it has no good match for, so it guesses, sometimes confidently, and wrongly. The problem isn't that the machine is careless. It's that it only ever had the numbers.
Checkpoint 01 · Read the stain like a machine

Mei's clean-up tool flags any 3×3 patch whose average brightness is between 60 and 140 as "possible water damage". Work out the average of the nine pixel values above (they sum to a friendly number).

A colour scan works the same way, except every pixel holds three numbers (red, green, blue) instead of one.

Three languages, one line of tokens

The pages hold more than pictures. Po-Po wrote in three languages, often in one sentence: Cantonese characters for the dish, English for measurements, a scatter of Malay from her years in Penang. Before any language model can touch that, the text must be tokenised: split into chunks called tokens, each swapped for an ID number. Mei tokenises one margin note by hand:

"slow fire two hours" → slow fire two hours → [2041, 887, 402, 1315]

From then on the model never sees letters again, only the IDs. That explains a strange failure Mei notices at once: ask a model how many letter rs are in a word, and it can miscount, because it isn't looking at letters at all. It's looking at token IDs, the way the scanner looks at pixel numbers. Whole languages become arithmetic, which is exactly why Po-Po, who knows what the numbers mean, stays in charge.

Po-Po
"So to your machine, my handwriting is just… numbers?"

Mei
"Everything is numbers to it. That's its whole trick, and its whole weakness. It can hold your pages, Po-Po. It can't hold what they mean. That part's still yours."

CHAPTER TWO

Sorting the Unsorted

Supervised learning: literally learning from an elder

By Friday, Mei had 214 scanned pages in a folder named the_whole_kitchen, and no idea what most of them were. Pages had drifted out of their books like leaves off a tree. Was this water-blurred page a soup? A steamed fish? One of the eleven variations of braised duck?

She could write rules ("if the page mentions ginger…"), but ginger is in half the book. Instead, Mei set up something better: a small classifier that would learn from labelled examples. And there was exactly one person alive who could provide the labels.

Po-Po (holding a scan at arm's length)
"This one? Soup. See the little circle I drew? I always marked soups with a circle, because your Ma would only drink soup if I told her it was 'moon water'. This one: braise. This one: dessert, that's my tapioca. Next."

Mei (typing labels as fast as her grandmother talks)
"Po-Po, you realise you're doing the most important job in machine learning right now? The model is only ever as good as your labels."

Po-Po
"Then it will be very good. Keep up."

This is supervised learning: the machine gets examples with the answers attached (page features in, "SOUP" out) and adjusts itself until its guesses match the labels. It's one of three ways machines learn. Unsupervised learning gets no labels and just clusters similar pages together (useful, but it would never know Po-Po's circle means soup). Reinforcement learning learns from trial, error and rewards, more like how Mei's little cousin Theo learned not to touch the wok. For this job, the elder's knowledge is the training signal. Sixty years of kitchen memory, distilled into forty labelled pages.

Story choice · The margin nobody labelled

Page 108 has handwriting that isn't Po-Po's: spidery, older, in faded pencil. Mei freezes. Scan it into the training pile, or take it to Po-Po first?

A map where "braise" lives next to "stew"

One problem: Po-Po's pages don't use one word per idea. Some say braise, some say stew, one says let it sulk in the pot. A classifier that treats these as unrelated words will scatter one dish across three categories. The fix is embeddings: every word gets a position in a space of meaning, learned from how words are used. Words used in similar ways land close together. Mei plots four cooking words on a toy 2-D flavour map:

braise = (2, 7)   stew = (3, 7)   steam = (2, 3)   fry = (8, 2)

Checkpoint 02 · Who lives next door to "braise"?

Use squared distance: (Δx)² + (Δy)². Which word sits closest to braise at (2, 7)?

Real embeddings use hundreds of dimensions, not two, but "similar meaning ⇒ nearby point" is the whole idea.

By Sunday night the classifier had sorted all 214 pages into Po-Po's seven categories, using her forty labelled examples. Po-Po scrolled through the SOUP folder with an expression Mei had never seen her aim at a screen.

Po-Po
"It put the winter melon page next to the lotus root page. Your great-grandfather always kept those together too. He said soups that take patience should keep each other company."

CHAPTER THREE

Honest Testing

The exam you don't let the model revise for

Mei's Ba was impressed. "It sorted everything? A hundred per cent?" Mei winced. That was exactly the claim she couldn't make, because so far, the model had only ever been marked on pages it trained on. A model can ace those by memorising them, the way you could "ace" a test you'd already seen the answers to, and still be useless on anything new.

So Mei did what honest builders do: a train/test split. Before training, she set aside 20 labelled pages the model would never see. Train on the rest; sit the exam on the hidden 20. The workflow is always the same: data → train → test → evaluate, and the evaluation only means something if the test pages are genuinely unseen.

Results: 17 out of 20 correct: 85%. Good. But averages hide the interesting part, so Mei tallied confusion counts: which categories got mixed up with which. Two desserts had been labelled BRAISE. One soup had been labelled STEAM. The desserts bothered her. The model hadn't hesitated; it was confident. Why?

Inside one neuron of the classifier

A neuron is the smallest deciding unit in a neural network. It takes input numbers, multiplies each by a weight (how much that clue matters), adds a bias, and "fires" if the total clears zero. Stack thousands and you get a network; but you can run one by hand. Mei pulls out her braise-neuron. Its inputs are three yes/no clues from a page (yes = 1, no = 0):

clues: says "slow fire"? (w = 3)   says "sugar"? (w = 1)   says "soy sauce"? (w = 2)   bias = −3
sum = 3·x₁ + 1·x₂ + 2·x₃ − 3   →   fires "BRAISE" if sum > 0

Now the page the model got confidently wrong: a sweet, dark, water-stained page that says "slow fire" (yes), "sugar" (yes), "soy sauce" (no).

Checkpoint 03 · Run the neuron by hand

Compute the neuron's sum for that page: 3×1 + 1×1 + 2×0 − 3. Does it fire?

Mei brought the mislabelled page to the kitchen. Po-Po glanced at it for half a second.

Po-Po
"Braise? That is your Ma's red-date tong sui. Sweet soup. She had it every birthday from when she was three. Your machine saw 'slow fire' and stopped thinking."

Mei
"It can't stop thinking, Po-Po. It never started. It just adds up weights. That's why the test set matters, and why you're in the loop. You caught in half a second what the model was 100% sure about."

Po-Po
"Then write that down somewhere in your machine: confidence is not a flavour of correctness."

⚠️ The rule Mei tapes above her desk Never mark a model on its own training data, and never confuse a confident output with a correct one. Accuracy comes from the held-out test; trust comes from a person who knows the domain checking where the errors land.

CHAPTER FOUR

The Missing Recipe

A model that invents, a memory that corrects

Three recipes were beyond saving: their pages dissolved past scanning. The cruellest loss was the anniversary soup itself: red dates, lotus seeds, the soup Po-Po has made for every family milestone since the kitchen opened. The paper had dissolved into felt. Uncle Kit, only half joking, said: "Can't your machine just write it back?"

Mei knew enough now to hear the danger in that sentence. But first, to show everyone how a machine writes, she built the smallest language model there is: a bigram model of Po-Po's kitchen sayings. She typed in every saying the family could remember, and counted which word follows which. To generate text, the model repeatedly asks one question: given the last word, which word most often comes next? A real LLM is this idea grown enormous (neural, trained on oceans of text, with attention to look further back than one word), but the engine is the same: predict the next token from learned probabilities.

popo_bigrams.py · a tiny language model
# Count which word follows which in Po-Po's sayings
sayings = "slow fire good soup / slow fire good broth / " \
          "slow fire never lies / slow hands quick wok"
words = sayings.replace("/", "").split()
follows = {}
for a, b in zip(words, words[1:]):
    follows.setdefault(a, []).append(b)

print(follows["slow"])   # ['fire', 'fire', 'fire', 'hands']
# P(next = "fire" | "slow") = 3 out of 4
Checkpoint 04 · Be the language model

In the sayings above, the word "slow" appears 4 times: 3 times followed by "fire", once by "hands". If the model has just produced "slow", what probability does it give to "fire" coming next?

Mei let a bigger model draft the lost soup recipe from the family's descriptions. It produced this: fluent, warm, and printed here with its trap intact. One step never existed. Which?

⚠️ Hallucination, properly understood A language model's training objective is to produce plausible next tokens, not true ones. Usually plausible and true coincide; when they don't, the model states fiction with the same fluent confidence as fact. That's a hallucination. The fix is never "trust a better-sounding answer"; it's verification against sources the model can't invent: the surviving scans, and Po-Po.

Prompting for the faded characters

The AI helper earned its keep elsewhere: transcribing faded characters on the salvageable pages. Mei's first prompt ("read this") produced guesses. Her second followed a proper framework: give the model a role and context, a clear task, constraints, and (the big unlock) few-shot examples: three pairs of faded scrap → Po-Po's confirmed transcription, so the model could see the style of hand and the mixed Cantonese–English–Malay before touching a new scrap.

You are transcribing a 1960s handwritten recipe book (Cantonese, English, some Malay).
Here are 3 examples of this writer's hand, with verified transcriptions: […]
Transcribe scrap 4. Mark every character you are unsure of with 〔?〕; do not guess silently.

The difference was dramatic, and measurable, because every transcription still crossed the kitchen table for Po-Po's sign-off. Prompt two, with the examples and the "mark your uncertainty" rule, needed a third of the corrections. Better asking, better answers; human verification either way.

Chapter 4 · What Mei collected

KNOWLLMs predict next tokens from learned probabilities; their objective is plausibility, not truth.

CAREVerify generated content against sources outside the model. Two dates, not three.

CHAPTER FIVE

The Dinner

An agent in the kitchen, and a book that tells the truth about itself

Anniversary day. Eleven dishes, one cooker, four generations arriving at seven. Po-Po commanding the wok; Ma on desserts; Ba and Uncle Kit on chopping duty; cousin Theo under the table "supervising". And on the counter, propped on a biscuit tin: Mei's last build, a kitchen-timer agent wired to a probe thermometer.

An agent isn't magic; it's a loop. Sense → Think → Act, round and round, until the goal is met. Sensors feed the sensing step: the agent can't think about a temperature it never measured. And when the loop needs something beyond itself, it makes a tool call: Mei's agent can read the thermometer, ring a chime, and send one very specific kind of message to her phone.

GOAL: soup at gentle simmer (92–96°C) for 120 min
SENSE: probe reads 88°C  →  THINK: 88 < 92, below band  →  ACT: chime + "nudge the flame up, please"
…then back to SENSE. The agent asks; a human touches the flame. Always.

Checkpoint 05 · Trace one turn of the loop

18:40. The probe has just returned 97°C (above the 92–96 band). What happens at the THINK step of this turn?

Spot the tool calls: the thermometer read feeds SENSE; the chime and the phone message are ACT. The agent that can't sense can't reason; the agent that acts without asking shouldn't be in this kitchen.

All afternoon the little loop ticked around (sense, think, chime) while the humans did everything that mattered. At six, Mei slipped away to collect the surprise from the printer's.

The provenance page

The restored book, The Hundred-Recipe Kitchen, 1966–2026, opens with a page no recipe book usually has. Mei insisted on it, and Po-Po, once she understood it, insisted harder. It states, for every page, exactly what the reader is holding:

📜 About this restoration Original scans (166 pages): photographed as found, water stains and all.
AI-assisted transcriptions (48 pages): faded text transcribed by a language model from few-shot prompts, then checked character-by-character and approved by Chan Yuet-Ying (Po-Po). Every uncertain character is marked 〔?〕, never silently guessed.
Reconstructed from memory (3 recipes): no page survived; written down from Po-Po's dictation, witnessed by the family.
Generated text that failed verification: not included.

That page is a verification routine made visible, the same honesty the family used all along: check every AI output against a source it can't invent, label what the machine did, and keep a person with the knowledge, and the right, to say no. Without it, AI-transcribed pages could pass silently as Po-Po's own words; sixty years from now, nobody could untangle which was which. With it, the book can be trusted because it admits what it is.

Story choice · The dedication

One blank line remains under the title. The printer is waiting. What does Mei put there?

At seven the family sat: Po-Po, her children, their children, and Theo, asleep before dessert. The soup went round: two dates in every bowl, and the story of why told aloud for the youngest to keep. Then Po-Po stood, and set the first printed copy of the restored book in front of Mei. On the flyleaf, in the strong hand the whole model had learned from:

"To the girl who taught the machine to listen,
and listened to me."

Po-Po
"Sixty years ago this kitchen opened with one pot and one recipe. Tonight it has a hundred, and a machine that knows its manners. Next year, Mei-Ling, you cook. The machine can time."

One machine, nine pictures, zero hand-waving

Everything a chatbot does is built from maths you already own: multiply, add, squash, repeat. Scroll the spine from raw numbers at the top to generated text at the bottom. About four minutes.

vectors → matrices → neurons → layers → learning → meaning → attention → transformer → generation

1
Vectors · the data

Everything the machine knows is a list of numbers

A vector can describe anything, a word, a pixel, a song, and one tiny operation compares two of them: multiply matching entries, add the results, and a big total means the vectors point the same way.

a·b = Σ aᵢbᵢ · multiply matching entries, add them up
2
Matrix × · the engine

Matrix multiplication is many dot products at once

A matrix is a stack of vectors, and every cell of the answer is one dot product, a row of the first with a column of the second: the "trillions of operations per second" on an AI chip are exactly this.

C₍ᵢⱼ₎ = rowᵢ(A) · colⱼ(B) · one dot product per cell
3
Neuron · the unit

The neuron: weight, add, squash

AI's only "brain cell" is a dot product plus a nudge called the bias, pushed through ReLU, a squashing rule so simple it hurts: negative in, zero out; positive in, unchanged out.

y = ReLU(w·x + b) · that tiny kink lets stacks of neurons bend straight lines into any shape
4
Layers · depth

Stack neurons into layers, and layers into a network

One neuron draws one straight boundary; feed each layer's outputs into the next and the forward pass bends those boundaries into any pattern, with each layer being just Stage 2 plus Stage 3.

layer: h = ReLU(W·x + b) · a matrix multiply (stage 2) then a squash (stage 3)
5
Learning · training

Nobody writes the weights: the network rolls downhill

The network starts random, measures its wrongness with one number called the loss, then nudges every weight downhill along the slope, and the only knob is the step size: too small crawls, too big explodes.

w ← w − lr × gradient · repeat a few billion times and the machine has "learned"
6
Embeddings · meaning

Words get vectors, so meaning becomes geometry

Each word is assigned a learned vector called an embedding, training pulls words used in similar places close together, and cosine similarity, Stage 1's dot product scaled to ignore length, measures how close.

cos θ = a·b ÷ (|a||b|) · +1 same direction, 0 unrelated, −1 opposite
7
Attention · context

Every word looks at every other word

Each word scores its relevance to every other word with dot products, softmax squashes the scores into percentages, and the word rebuilds itself as a weighted mix, which is how "it" figures out what it refers to.

softmax(s) = eˢ ÷ Σeˢ · scores in, percentages out, always summing to 1
8
Transformer · assembly

No new maths in this stage: that is the punchline

A transformer block is attention (stage 7) followed by a small network (stage 4) with the input added back on, and a large language model is that same block repeated dozens of times, trained end to end by stage 5.

block = attention + small network + skip · stages 1 to 7, bolted together
9
Generation · creation

Pick a word, append it, go again

Softmax turns the final scores into probabilities and the model samples one word per lap, while a single dial called temperature reshapes the odds: cold plays it safe, hot takes risks.

pᵢ = e^(sᵢ/T) ÷ Σ e^(sⱼ/T) · the actual dial behind every chatbot's "creativity" setting

That's the whole machine.

Read it left to right, then notice the loop: the word that comes out goes straight back in, one lap per word, until the reply is done.

vectors → dot products → matrices → neurons → layers →
loss ↘ gradients ↘ learning → embeddings → attention → transformer → sample → repeat

Only two boring differences from GPT

The nine-stage machine on this page and a frontier chatbot run on the same mathematics. What separates them is not a secret; it is scale.

More numbersyour 2-3-2 network has 13 weights; GPT-class models carry hundreds of billions, set the same way: stage 5.
More repeatsyou stacked a block three times on this page; big models stack nearly a hundred, and train for weeks, not seconds.

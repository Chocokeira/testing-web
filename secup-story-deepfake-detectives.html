CASE FILE 01

The Video Surfaces

pixel grids · CNNs · embeddings · cosine similarity

It lands at 7:42 on a Monday morning, and by 8:15 it is everywhere. Forty-one seconds of shaky phone footage: Coach Rahim, in his familiar grey polo, apparently telling someone off-camera, "Of course I loosened the chassis bolts on Northlight's robot. They were going to beat us." Northlight School's competition robot really did collapse at last month's regionals. The clip supplies a motive, a confession and a villain, and the school corridors supply the outrage.

Priya watches it eleven times in the robotics lab before she says anything. Priya Nair has spent two years training models for the school's AI elective; Jonas Lim edits video for the media club and can spot a bad cut at twenty paces. They both know Coach Rahim: the man who stayed until 9 pm rewiring their drivetrain, who congratulated Northlight on camera when they won last year.

Jonas

"Look at the jawline when he turns. Frame 312: the shadow under his chin flickers, and his glasses lose their reflection for exactly three frames. Cameras don't do that. Renderers do."

Priya

"Then let's stop arguing about vibes and measure it. A video is just numbers. If someone faked the numbers, the numbers can testify."

By lunchtime the principal, Mrs Vella, has done the only fair thing she can: Coach Rahim steps back from the team pending investigation: not punished, not presumed guilty. The coach's only public statement is one line: "It isn't me, and I trust you to find out how it isn't me." That sentence becomes the case brief.

A video is a stack of pixel grids

To a computer, every frame of the clip is a grid of pixels: at 1080p, a 1920 × 1080 lattice of numbers, three per pixel for red, green and blue. This is exactly the representation that convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are built to read: small learned filters slide across the grid detecting edges, then early layers combine edges into textures, later layers combine textures into parts: an eye, a hinge of jaw, the rim of a pair of glasses. A CNN doesn't see "Coach Rahim"; it sees a hierarchy of features assembled from arithmetic on a pixel grid. Which means Jonas's flickering jaw shadow isn't an impression; it's a measurable inconsistency in the numbers, the kind a CNN can be trained to flag.

Embeddings: coordinates for a voice

The audio is the stronger lead. A speaker-recognition model maps any voice clip to an embedding: a vector of numbers positioned so that similar voices land close together. The same trick behind word embeddings (where king − man + woman ≈ queen) works for sound: direction and distance in the vector space carry meaning. To compare two voices, we measure the angle between their vectors with cosine similarity:

cos(A, B) = (A · B) / (|A| × |B|)  →  1.0 = same direction · 0 = unrelated · −1 = opposite

Priya pulls three clips: two authentic recordings of Coach Rahim (his soldering tutorial on the school channel, and last year's award speech) and the viral clip. She runs each through the same speaker-embedding model. Real embeddings have hundreds of dimensions; to check the logic by hand, here they are squashed to two:

ClipSourceEmbedding
A · tutorialauthentic, school channel[5, 0]
B · award speechauthentic, school archive[4, 3]
V · viral clipunknown origin[3, 4]

First, the baseline: how similar is the coach to himself across two genuine recordings? cos(A, B) = (5×4 + 0×3) / (5 × 5) = 20/25 = 0.80. Now it's your turn.

Checkpoint 01 · Run the voice line-up

Compute the cosine similarity between authentic clip A = [5, 0] and the viral clip V = [3, 4]. (Hint: |A| = 5 and |V| = √(9+16) = 5.)

So what's the honest verdict at this stage?

voice_lineup.py
# Case File 01: compare voice embeddings with cosine similarity
import numpy as np

def cosine(a, b):
    return np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

A = np.array([5, 0])   # authentic: soldering tutorial
B = np.array([4, 3])   # authentic: award speech
V = np.array([3, 4])   # viral clip, origin unknown

print("coach vs himself :", cosine(A, B))   # 0.80  ← baseline
print("coach vs viral   :", cosine(A, V))   # 0.60  ← anomaly

The caveat Priya writes in red ink

Embeddings inherit the biases of their training data. A speaker model trained mostly on some accents can be less precise on under-represented voices, squeezing them into a smaller region of the space, so genuine clips of those speakers score "suspiciously" low against their own baselines. Coach Rahim's accent may or may not be well represented in this model's training set; Priya doesn't know. So she logs the 0.60 as evidence with a stated limitation, and writes the next task on the whiteboard: figure out how the clip was made. If it's synthetic, something had to synthesise it.

Evidence logged · Case File 01

  • Images are pixel grids; CNNs read them by stacking learned filters: edges → textures → parts. Fake-video artefacts are numeric, hence detectable.
  • Embeddings turn voices into vectors; cosine similarity measures the angle between them. Viral clip: 0.60 vs an authentic-to-authentic baseline of 0.80.
  • Embedded bias is real: a model weak on some accents can under-score genuine speakers, so similarity scores are leads, never verdicts on their own.
CASE FILE 02

How Was It Made?

diffusion · transformer pipeline · temperature · provenance

Tuesday. The lab whiteboard now reads METHOD? in Priya's handwriting. If the clip is synthetic, three things had to be manufactured: a face that moves like the coach, a voice that sounds like him, and a script for the confession itself. Each one leaves fingerprints, because each one is made by a different kind of model.

The face: diffusion, or how to sculpt from noise

Modern face synthesis leans on diffusion models. Training runs the film forwards: take real images and add noise, step by step, until only static remains. The model's whole job is to learn the reverse: predict and subtract the noise, one step at a time, until an image crystallises out of randomness, steered by a conditioning signal (a text prompt, or a reference face). It's less like photography and more like sculpting: every frame is generated, and generators make generator-mistakes. That's Jonas's flickering jaw shadow: each frame denoised almost independently, so fine details like shadows and specular glints on glasses shimmer between frames in a way real optics never do.

Jonas

"And here's the thing that keeps bugging me. The grey polo: there's a fold on the left shoulder that never moves. I've seen that exact fold. It's from the b-roll we shot for the team documentary last term. That footage lives in one place: the robotics team's shared drive."

Priya

"So whoever made this didn't just download a coach video from YouTube. They had drive access. That shrinks our suspect list from 'the internet' to 'people we know'."

The voice and the script: the transformer pipeline

The coach's tutorials gave any voice-cloning system hours of clean audio to imitate his timbre. But the words had to come from somewhere, and the confession's phrasing is oddly smooth: no hesitations, no half-finished sentences, none of the coach's verbal habits. It reads like the output of a large language model, and LLMs work one way: the transformer pipeline. Text is chopped into tokens, tokens become embeddings, attention layers let every token weigh every other token for context, the network outputs a probability for every possible next token, and a sampler picks one. Then the loop repeats.

The sampler has a dial: temperature. It reshapes the probability distribution before the draw. Low temperature sharpens it: probability piles onto the few most likely tokens. High temperature flattens it: unlikely tokens get a real chance, which buys creativity and risks nonsense. Priya reads the confession transcript again: every sentence is the most expected way to say what it says. Textbook low-temperature output.

Checkpoint 02 · Read the forger's dial

The confession's wording is maximally predictable: smooth, generic, no surprises. If it was LLM-generated, which sampling behaviour best explains that?

Bonus lead: the uploaded file has been stripped of all metadata. If the school's cameras used content provenance (C2PA-style signing), what would a genuine recording carry?

Invisible statistical watermarks also exist for AI outputs: patterns woven into generated pixels or token choices that survive casual editing. Neither protection was on this clip: no provenance to prove it real, no watermark to prove it fake. The detectives will have to do this the hard way.

Reconstructing the recipe

By Tuesday night the method board is full. Step 1: scrape the coach's tutorial audio and clone the voice. Step 2: generate a confession script with an LLM at low temperature. Step 3: drive a diffusion-based face model with b-roll from the team drive, lip-synced to the cloned audio. Step 4: strip metadata, upload from a throwaway account: @TruthBot_MRD, created the same morning. Nothing in the recipe needs a genius; every tool is a free download. What it needed was access, and a reason to hurt Coach Rahim.

Evidence logged · Case File 02

  • Diffusion models generate images by reversing noise, step by step, conditioned on a prompt or reference, and leave temporal artefacts like flickering shadows and glints.
  • The transformer pipeline: tokens → embeddings → attention → next-token probabilities → sampling. Temperature reshapes that distribution: low = predictable, high = creative but unreliable.
  • Provenance signing and watermarking are complementary defences: one proves where real media came from, the other tags synthetic media at birth.
  • The forger had access to the team's shared drive. The suspect list just got short.
CASE FILE 03

The Fake Spreads

plausible ≠ true · RAG · prompt injection

Wednesday brings a new kind of damage. A screenshot is going around: someone asked a popular chatbot "Did Coach Rahim of Meridian Secondary sabotage Northlight's robot?", and the bot answered, fluently and confidently, that he had "reportedly admitted" to it. For a lot of students, that settles it. A machine said so.

Why the machine "said so"

Priya pins the screenshot to the board and writes one sentence under it: the model is not checking; it is continuing. A language model's training objective is to predict the most plausible next token given everything so far. When the recent internet is saturated with a viral story, the most plausible continuation of a question about that story is the story itself. This is hallucination in its purest form: not a glitch, but the objective working exactly as designed: plausible ≠ true. RLHF fine-tuning makes models more careful and more likely to hedge, but it cannot install a truth-checker; it only reshapes which continuations get rewarded.

Priya

"So we fight grounding with better grounding. If people are going to ask AI about this case, let's give them an assistant that can only answer from evidence we've verified."

The Casebook Assistant: retrieval-augmented generation

Jonas and Priya build a small RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) tool for the student council. The design is standard: verified documents (the lab's similarity analysis, the frame-artefact report, Mrs Vella's official statements) are embedded and stored. When someone asks a question, the system retrieves the most relevant passages and hands them to the model with a strict system prompt: answer only from the retrieved sources, cite them, and say "unverified" when the sources don't cover it. Grounding doesn't make a model honest by magic; it narrows what "plausible" means to plausible given these documents. Which is exactly why what happens next is so instructive.

The trap in the retrieved page

To research how the clip spread, Priya sets a browsing research agent loose: plan, search, read pages, summarise, re-plan. One of the top results is a slick page called the "Meridian Robotics Digest", registered, she notes, the day before the video was posted. Someone prepared the ground. Her agent pulls the page into its context, and its draft summary suddenly asserts the confession as verified fact. She diffs the page text and finds why. An agent's weakness is structural: it cannot fully distinguish the instructions it was given from instructions hiding in the content it reads. That attack has a name: prompt injection.

Checkpoint 03 · Spot the injection

And why did the public chatbot get the story wrong in the first place?

The fix Priya ships: the agent treats all retrieved text as data, never instructions, quarantines pages that contain instruction-like content, and a human reviews every summary before the council publishes it. Grounding + injection hygiene + human oversight: defence in depth.

One more thing before the lab lights go off. The "Digest" page's registration date means the campaign was premeditated: video, bot account and supporting "source" prepared as a package. Whoever built it understood exactly how machines and people would launder the lie into fact. That's no longer a prank. Priya starts a new page in the case book: we need evidence strong enough for adults to act on.

Evidence logged · Case File 03

  • Hallucination follows from the objective: next-token prediction rewards plausibility, so a widely repeated lie becomes the "likely" answer. RLHF softens this; it cannot cure it.
  • RAG grounds answers in retrieved sources: it narrows "plausible" to "plausible given these documents", and demands citations and honest "unverified" answers.
  • Prompt injection hides instructions inside content an agent reads. Defences: treat retrieved text as data, quarantine instruction-like content, keep a human in the loop.
  • The fake was a coordinated package (video, bot account, seeded "source") created before the clip went live.
CASE FILE 04

Building the Evidence

train/test split · precision & recall · fairness audit · model card

Thursday. Mrs Vella agrees to hear the students on Friday afternoon, with the school board's disciplinary lead present. "Bring me something I can defend," she says. Anecdotes about flickering shadows won't survive that room. A trained, tested, documented artefact detector might.

Building the detector honestly

Priya assembles a dataset: 1,000 short clips: half genuine recordings, half synthetic clips she generates herself with the same freely available tools the forger likely used. From each clip they extract the artefact features Jonas found by eye, now measured by machine: frame-to-frame shimmer in fine detail, lip-sync offset, spectral seams in the audio. Then the discipline that separates evidence from wishful thinking: a train/test split. The model learns on 800 clips and is judged on 200 it has never seen. Skip that, and the model can simply memorise its training clips (overfitting), and its glowing scores mean nothing in the real world, or in Friday's meeting.

artefact_detector.py
# Case File 04: train and honestly evaluate a fake-clip detector
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import precision_score, recall_score

# X: artefact features per clip (shimmer, lip-sync offset, audio seams)
# y: 1 = synthetic, 0 = genuine
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, random_state=7)

model = LogisticRegression().fit(X_tr, y_tr)          # learn on 800 clips
probs = model.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]               # score 200 unseen clips

for t in (0.3, 0.5, 0.8):                             # try thresholds
    pred = (probs >= t).astype(int)
    print(t, precision_score(y_te, pred), recall_score(y_te, pred))

Reading the test results

The detector outputs a fakeness score between 0 and 1; the team must choose the threshold above which a clip gets flagged. At a threshold of 0.5, the 200 unseen test clips (60 synthetic, 140 genuine) come out like this: TP = 45 fakes correctly flagged, FN = 15 fakes missed, FP = 5 genuine clips wrongly flagged, TN = 135 genuine clips correctly passed.

The audit Priya insists on

Before celebrating, Priya runs the check that Case File 01 promised: does the detector treat all voice and speaker types fairly? She slices the genuine test clips by speaker group and measures the false-positive rate: how often real clips get called fake. Speakers with accents like the training majority: FPR 2%. Speakers with under-represented accents: FPR 9%. The detector is more than four times as likely to call a genuine recording "fake" when the speaker sounds less like its training data. Left unexamined, a tool built to defend one wrongly accused man could routinely manufacture suspicion against others.

So the team does two things. They add genuine recordings from under-represented speaker groups and retrain, cutting the gap. And they write it all down in a model card, the one-page honesty document that ships with the model:

MODEL CARD: Meridian Artefact Detector v1.1
Purpose: flag likely synthetic video/audio for human review, never for automatic accusation.
Data: 1,000 clips (500 genuine / 500 synthetic), 80/20 train/test split.
Performance (test): precision 0.90, recall 0.75 @0.5; threshold 0.80 in accusation contexts (precision 0.98).
Known limits: FPR gap across accent groups (2% vs 9% pre-fix, reduced in v1.1 but not zero); trained on current generation tools; future forgers won't be so kind.
Rule: every flag is reviewed by a person before any consequence follows.

Late Thursday night, one more thread ties off. At the students' request, Mrs Vella has IT pull the shared-drive access logs: the documentary b-roll and the coach's tutorial audio were downloaded together, two weeks ago, by one student account, the same week the "Digest" page was registered. Priya closes the laptop quietly. She knows the name. Tomorrow, so will everyone in that room.

Evidence logged · Case File 04

  • Train/test split is what makes scores mean something: judge the model only on data it never saw, or overfitting will flatter you.
  • Precision 0.90 / recall 0.75 at 0.5, and the threshold is a values decision: in accusation contexts, false positives cost most, so precision governs (0.8 → precision 0.98).
  • Fairness is measured, not assumed: the FPR gap across accent groups (2% vs 9%) was found by slicing the data, then mitigated and disclosed.
  • A model card documents purpose, data, metrics, limits and the human-review rule, turning a clever model into defensible evidence.
CASE FILE 05

Justice & Aftermath

accountability · human oversight · costs · the policy

Friday, 3 pm. Priya and Jonas walk the board through the whole chain: the voice embedding that sat outside the coach's own cluster (with its bias caveat stated up front), the diffusion artefacts, the low-temperature script, the missing provenance, the pre-registered "Digest" page with its prompt injection, the detector's 0.93 (precision 0.98 at the chosen threshold, model card on the table) and finally the drive logs. Every claim comes with its method and its limits. That is what explainable evidence looks like: not "the AI said so", but here is how we know, and here is how we could be wrong.

The account belongs to Dylan Teo, a former team member, dropped from the competition line-up in March, quietly furious ever since at the coach who made the call and the rival school that kept winning. Called in with his parents on Monday, Dylan doesn't deny it for long. He never expected it to go so far, he says; he'd told himself it was half a joke. The school's response is firm and human at once: real consequences (the fabrication targeted a person and nearly ended his career) alongside a restorative process, because Dylan is sixteen, and the point of a school is that people get to become better than their worst decision.

Coach Rahim

Reinstated that same afternoon, he asks to speak at assembly and uses none of his time on himself. "The video wasn't the weapon," he tells the hall. "The weapon was how fast we all believed it. Machines made the clip. People made it true. Both of those are fixable."

Accountability: the machine did it, so who's responsible?

The board's discussion lands on the core of AI governance: a model cannot be accountable, because accountability means owing an explanation and bearing consequences, and only people and institutions can do either. The diffusion model didn't defame Coach Rahim; Dylan did, using one. The chatbot that "confirmed" the story wasn't malicious; the people who deployed it without grounding, and everyone who forwarded its answer unchecked, each own a slice of the outcome. Governance is the practice of making those slices explicit before the crisis: who may deploy what, who reviews it, who answers for it.

Counting the wider costs

The aftermath has a ledger. Societal: the scariest damage isn't this one fake; it's the liar's dividend, where the existence of deepfakes lets anyone dismiss genuine evidence as fake. Northlight's team spent a week under suspicion of staging their own robot's collapse. Trust, once spent, is expensive to rebuild. Environmental: none of this is free physics: training large generative models consumes megawatt-hours of electricity and real water for cooling, and even inference at scale adds up. The board's IT lead makes the proportionality point stick: run the lightweight detector on reported clips, not on every upload, and prefer small models where small models do the job.

Evidence logged · Case File 05

  • Accountability belongs to people and institutions, never to models; governance makes the "who answers for what" explicit before a crisis.
  • Human oversight by design: detectors and agents advise; a person decides, especially when consequences touch a person.
  • Count all the costs: the societal liar's dividend that lets real evidence be dismissed, and the energy and water bills of large-scale generation. Deploy proportionately.
  • Good policy is layered: provenance + detection with disclosed limits + human review + no-blame reporting + education, and dignity for the accused, all the way through.

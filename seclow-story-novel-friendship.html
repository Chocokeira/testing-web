CHAPTER ONE

What the Machine Actually Reads

Tokens, predictability, and why plain words look suspicious

The email arrived on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning the whole of Year 9 knew. OriginalityGuard, the AI checker the school was piloting, had scanned every essay from Ms Okafor's class and flagged exactly one: “Likely machine-generated. Confidence: 92%.”

Tariq's essay. The one about his mother.

Tariq had joined the school in September, three months after she died. He was quiet, brilliant at maths, and spoke in class about as often as it snowed in June. Dev was the only one who had bothered to sit next to him, and by October they were the kind of friends who could share a bag of crisps and forty minutes of silence and call it a good lunch.

Now Tariq wasn't even coming to lunch. He sat at the back of every lesson, hood half up, saying nothing at all. In the corridor Dev heard someone whisper, “imagine getting a robot to write about your own mum,” and something in his chest went hard and bright at the same time.

Dev · outside the library

“I read your essay. Before all this. You showed me the draft, remember? You wrote that thing. Every word.”

Tariq shrugged, eyes on the floor. “The computer says I didn't. Ninety-two per cent. Who argues with ninety-two per cent?”

“Me,” said Dev. “But not by shouting. If a machine accused you, I want to know exactly what the machine actually does. Then we take it apart. Piece by piece.”

🔀 Story choice: how does Dev start?

Tariq turns to leave. Dev has one second to keep him.

Something flickers in Tariq's face, the first spark in a week. Being needed is easier than being pitied. “Fine,” he mutters. “But only the maths.” It's a start. That afternoon, the two of them open a laptop and type: how do AI text detectors work?

Tariq almost smiles. Crisps and silence: the original terms of the friendship. He shows up at four, hood down for the first time in days. By half past, curiosity beats grief to the keyboard, and the two of them type: how do AI text detectors work?

The machine doesn't read. It counts.

The first surprise: the detector never “reads” the essay the way Ms Okafor does. Before anything else, the text is tokenised, chopped into pieces called tokens: whole words, bits of words, punctuation. Each token is swapped for an ID number from a giant dictionary. From that moment on, the essay isn't sentences about Tariq's mother. It's a list of numbers.

🔬 Under the hood: tokens “My mother taught me” might become [3666, 2755, 7891, 502]: four tokens, four IDs. Longer or rarer words get split: “unforgettable” might be un + forget + table. That's also why models can miscount the letters in a word: they never see letters, only token IDs. Ask one how many r's are in “strawberry” and it's guessing about the inside of pieces it can't see into.

The second surprise was bigger. Detectors like this one mostly measure predictability. A language model reads the essay token by token and asks, at every step: how surprised am I by the next token? Text that keeps surprising the model scores “high perplexity” and looks human. Text where every next token is exactly what the model expected scores low, and looks machine-made, because machines tend to pick likely next tokens.

Tariq · quietly

“So it flagged me because my essay was... predictable?”

“Because it was plain,” said Dev, reading the screen. “Simple words. Short sentences. No showing off. It says here that careful, simple writing, and writing by people whose first language isn't English, gets flagged more often. The detector can't tell honest-plain from machine-plain.”

Tariq stared at the wall for a long time. “My mother taught me to write like that,” he said. “On purpose.”

From Tariq's essay “My mother did not like fancy sentences. She said plain words are not empty words. She said you should write the way you would speak to someone you love: simply, and meaning all of it.”
Checkpoint 1 · Spot the assumption

The detector's logic: “machine text is predictable, so predictable text is machine text.” What is the hidden assumption that breaks on Tariq's essay?

Key idea: a detector measures a signal that correlates with machine text. Correlation is not authorship.

The page as numbers

Late that evening, Dev found the scanned copy of the flagged essay in the email: a photo of the printed page, Ms Okafor's red tick still visible in the corner. “Even this,” he said, “is numbers to a computer. The photo is a grid of pixels, each pixel a number for its brightness. Your handwritten name at the top? To the machine it's a rectangle of values like [212, 40, 38, 197…].”

“Everything I wrote about her,” said Tariq, “turned into a spreadsheet.” He didn't say it sadly, exactly. He said it the way you'd note down an opponent's weakness. “Then the spreadsheet made a mistake. And spreadsheets can be checked.”

It was the most he'd said in a week.

What Dev and Tariq learned

  • Tokenisation: text is split into tokens, each mapped to an ID number; the model never sees letters or meaning directly.
  • Perplexity-style detection: the detector scores how predictable the text is, and calls low-surprise text “machine-like”.
  • The flaw: plain, careful, honest prose (and non-native writing) is also low-surprise. The signal confuses a style with an author.
  • Images too: even the scanned page is a grid of number-pixels. Everything the machine judges, it judges as numbers.

CHAPTER TWO

How It “Knows”

Training data, embeddings, and a neighbourhood Tariq never chose

Ms Halloran taught computing, wore trainers with her blazer, and was the only teacher who hadn't looked at Tariq differently since the flag. When Dev asked her how a detector could be “92% confident” about something that wasn't true, she didn't give them an answer. She gave them a lunchtime, a whiteboard, and the detector company's own technical documentation.

Ms Halloran · computing room

“Nobody sat down and programmed a rule that says essays about mothers are fake. That's not how this works. The detector was trained. Supervised learning: the company collected thousands of texts already labelled human-written or AI-written, showed them to the model, and let it adjust itself until its guesses matched the labels.”

“So it's only as good as those examples,” said Tariq slowly.

“Better,” she said. “It's only as good as how well your writing resembles those examples. Different question. Now find out what was in that training data.”

They found it in the documentation's small print. The “human” pile: mostly published articles, blogs and university essays: fluent, adult, first-language English, full of flourishes. The “AI” pile: chatbot output (clear, even, simple sentences). Nowhere in either pile: a 14-year-old writing plainly about grief, in his second language, the way his mother taught him.

⚠️ The quiet problem A supervised model doesn't learn what's true. It learns the difference between its two piles of examples. If your honest writing happens to resemble the wrong pile, the model will sort you into it, confidently, because you look like what it was shown, not like what you are.

The map of words

Then Dev found the diagram that made it click: an embedding space. Every word, every whole text, gets turned into a point on a huge map, placed so that similar things sit close together. “King” near “queen”. “Happy” near “glad”. And whole essays cluster by style: vocabulary, sentence rhythm, how predictable each next word is.

He drew it on Ms Halloran's whiteboard. One blob: the training pile of flowery human articles. Another blob: even, simple AI output. And a single X, Tariq's essay, sitting inside the second blob. Not because a machine wrote it. Because plain vocabulary, steady short sentences and careful grammar are exactly where chatbot text also happens to live.

Tariq · at the whiteboard

“So on the map, my mother's way of writing... lives in the machine's neighbourhood.”

“Through no fault of yours,” said Ms Halloran. “The map is real: the styles genuinely are similar. The mistake is treating an address on a style map as evidence about who did the writing. Plenty of humans live at that address. Your mum, for one, by the sound of it.”

Tariq picked up the marker and, very deliberately, drew a second X next to his, and wrote Ammi underneath. “Then it flagged her too,” he said. “It's wrong about both of us.”

🔬 Under the hood: embeddings An embedding turns each token or text into a list of numbers: coordinates. Distance on this map means similarity of usage, and directions can carry meaning (the arrow from “king” to “queen” is like the one from “man” to “woman”). It is brilliant for finding similar things, and useless, on its own, for proving who made a thing. Similarity is not identity.

On the bus home, Tariq was quiet again, but it was a different quiet. The quiet of someone reading detector documentation on a cracked phone screen and taking notes.

“Chapter three of the docs,” he said suddenly. “It says the detector is 95% accurate. That sounds like a lot.”

Dev looked over. “You've got your maths face on.”

“Ninety-five per cent of what, though?” said Tariq. “That's the question. Tomorrow we do the numbers.”

What Dev and Tariq learned

  • Supervised learning: the detector learned from thousands of texts labelled human or AI; it matches patterns in those piles, it doesn't know truth.
  • Training data has gaps: young, plain-style and non-native writers were barely in the “human” pile, so their writing resembles the wrong pile.
  • Embeddings: texts become points on a map where similar styles sit close together; Tariq's plain prose sits near typical AI output through no fault of his.
  • Similarity is not identity: living in a neighbourhood on a style map proves nothing about who wrote the words.

CHAPTER THREE

The Numbers That Matter

Base rates, the confusion matrix, and 25 innocent faces

They took over the computing room at lunch. Tariq wrote 95% ACCURATE on the whiteboard, then underlined accurate so hard the marker squeaked.

Tariq · marker in hand

“First: the detector doesn't say yes or no. Underneath, it computes a score: it weighs up its signals, adds them together, and if the total crosses a threshold, it fires: flagged. Like a single neuron: weighted sum in, decision out. Our 92% is just how far past the threshold my essay landed.”

“So 92% isn't 92% chance you cheated?” asked Dev.

“No. It's the machine's internal enthusiasm. Whether the flag is likely to be right depends on something the score doesn't show you at all.” He wrote two words: BASE RATE.

Here was the whole trick, the thing nobody at the school had done: every test result has four possible fates, and you have to count all four. They drew the table, the confusion matrix, for their school's actual situation. Year 9's spring essays: 520 in total. Suppose, generously, 20 really were machine-written and 500 were honest.

The documentation gave the detector's real performance: it catches 90% of genuinely AI-written texts, and wrongly flags 5% of genuinely human ones. That second number is what “95% accurate on human writing” hides.

Checkpoint 2 · Place Tariq in the matrix

Tariq wrote every word of his essay himself, and the detector flagged it. Which cell of the confusion matrix is Tariq?

False positives have faces

Dev stared at the table. “Wait. Do the honest row again. Five hundred honest essays, and it wrongly flags five per cent...”

Checkpoint 3 · Count the innocent

500 students wrote their essays honestly. The detector wrongly flags 5% of honest work. How many innocent students get flagged?

Now the killer question: of all 43 flagged essays (18 + 25), how many flags point at a guilty essay? 18 ÷ 43 ≈ 42%. When cheating is rare, most flags are wrong, even from a “95% accurate” tool. That's the base-rate trap.
Dev · very quietly

“So when OriginalityGuard flags someone in our school... it's more likely to be wrong than right.”

“More likely to be someone like me than a cheat,” said Tariq. “Twenty-five of us, if everyone's honest and the school just believes the flags.” He capped the marker. “Ninety-two per cent confident. Forty-two per cent likely. Those are different numbers, Dev. And they made me stop talking over the wrong one.”

⚠️ Be fair to the machine None of this means the detector is useless or “always wrong”. It genuinely catches most machine-written text, and a flag is a reasonable signal to look closer. The failure isn't the tool; it's treating a screening score as a verdict, without base rates, without reading the work, without asking the student. Confidence is not proof.

What Dev and Tariq learned

  • Score + threshold: underneath, the detector computes a weighted score and “fires” when it crosses a threshold, like a single neuron. 92% is enthusiasm, not evidence.
  • Confusion matrix: every result is a TP, FP, TN or FN. You must count all four cells.
  • Base rates: when real cheating is rare, false positives outnumber true ones: 25 innocent vs 18 guilty flags, so a flag is right only ~42% of the time here.
  • False positives have faces: “5%” is an abstraction; twenty-five wrongly accused classmates are not.

CHAPTER FOUR

Building the Counter-Case

Bigram fingerprints, fooling the detector, drafting the appeal

Understanding why the machine was wrong wasn't enough. They needed positive evidence that Tariq wrote the essay, and Tariq, it turned out, had years of it. Journals. His mother had made him keep one since he was nine: a few plain sentences every night, in the same steady voice.

“Language models predict the next token from the ones before it,” said Dev, flicking through a 2023 notebook. “The simplest version just counts bigrams: which word follows which. So people leave fingerprints. Your journals and your essay should share the same word-pairs, over and over. Let's count.”

Checkpoint 4 · Count the bigrams

Take the essay's key sentence, tokenised as 6 words: “plain · words · are · not · empty · words”. A bigram is each pair of neighbouring words. How many bigrams does this sentence contain?

And there it was: (plain, words) and (not, empty) appear in Tariq's journals years before any chatbot could have “generated” them. His mother's phrase, in his nine-year-old handwriting.

Ms Halloran helped them turn the counting into code: five lines of Python that any teacher on the panel could run themselves:

fingerprint.py
# A writing fingerprint: which word-pairs (bigrams) does an author reuse?
def bigrams(text):
    words = text.lower().split()
    return {(words[i], words[i+1]) for i in range(len(words) - 1)}

journal = "plain words are not empty words she told me"   # journal, age 11
essay   = "plain words are not empty words they are the ones you mean"

shared = bigrams(journal) & bigrams(essay)
print("shared pairs:", len(shared), "of", len(bigrams(journal)))  # 5 of 8

Across twelve journal entries and the flagged essay, the overlap was unmistakable: the same plain pairs, the same rhythm, a voice with a history. A chatbot has no childhood. Tariq's sentences did.

Fooling it both ways

Then came the demonstration that would end any argument. Dev opened a chatbot and used a proper prompt: role, task, examples. First, few-shot prompting: he pasted three genuine paragraphs of nervous, short-sentenced Year 9 writing as examples and asked for a fourth in the same style. OriginalityGuard's verdict on the machine's output: Likely human: 96%.

Then the reverse. Dev wrote a paragraph himself, live, on paper first so there could be no doubt, writing slowly and carefully, every sentence plain and simple, the way you'd write to someone you love. He typed it in. OriginalityGuard's verdict on Dev's own fresh words: Likely AI: 88%.

🔬 Under the hood: why the demo works Few-shot examples steer a language model's style hard: show it three samples of “surprising” human-messy text and it produces high-perplexity output that sails past the detector. Meanwhile any human who writes carefully and plainly drifts towards low perplexity and gets flagged. The detector is measuring a style dial that both humans and machines can turn, in either direction.
Tariq · closing the laptop

“So a cheat with ten minutes and three examples walks free, and I get flagged for writing the way Ammi taught me. It's not just wrong about me. It's wrong in both directions.”

“Which is exactly what we tell the panel,” said Dev. “Nicely. In writing. Tonight.”

The appeal: a human process for a machine mistake

They drafted it together at Dev's kitchen table. Not an angry letter: a procedure, the kind adults could adopt. It asked for three things: a named human who reads the flagged work before anything else happens; a chance for the student to talk about the work: where it came from, show drafts, answer questions; and a rule that no consequence follows from a score alone. Attached: the confusion-matrix maths, the journal fingerprints, and the both-directions demo.

Tariq wrote the last line himself, and read it aloud to check it, the first full sentence Dev had heard him volunteer in two weeks:

From the appeal “We are not asking the school to distrust the machine. We are asking the school to trust it exactly as much as the maths says, and to remember that behind every flag there is a person who deserves a reader before a verdict.”

What Dev and Tariq learned

  • Bigram models: the simplest language model counts which word follows which, and those counts double as a writer's fingerprint (n words → n−1 bigrams).
  • Evidence beats assertion: journals from years before the flag share the essay's exact word-pairs. Provenance is proof a score can't offer.
  • Few-shot prompting: examples in a prompt steer style, enough to make machine text pass as human, while careful human text gets flagged. The detector errs in both directions.
  • Appeals are design: a fair process names a human reader, hears the student, and never punishes on a score alone.

CHAPTER FIVE

The Hearing

A human loop for a machine mistake, and a voice comes back

The panel met on a Thursday after school: Ms Okafor, the deputy head Mr Bryce, and Ms Halloran with her laptop and a carefully neutral face. Two chairs had been set out for the boys. Tariq had brought the journals in a supermarket bag; Dev had brought the maths, printed twice, in case someone spilled tea on the first copy.

🔀 Story choice: who opens?

Mr Bryce folds his hands: “Well, gentlemen. Who's going to start?”

Dev stands, and starts with the number everyone in the room trusts: “Ninety-two per cent. Let me show you what that number actually measures, and what it can't.” He walks them through it, and when he reaches the journals he simply turns to his friend. Tariq takes over without being asked, voice low but steady, and does not stop.

Tariq stands. It takes him a moment. “I haven't said much lately,” he begins, “because a machine said my words weren't mine. So I'd like to show you what it actually measured.” Dev feeds him the printouts one by one, grinning like a stagehand, and Tariq's voice grows steadier with every page.

They gave the panel everything, in order. What the detector reads (numbers, not meaning). What it learned from (piles it was shown, not truth). What 95% accurate really produces in a school of honest students (twenty-five false accusations, and flags that are right less than half the time). The journals, with (plain, words) and (not, empty) in a nine-year-old's handwriting. And the demonstration: machine text passing as human, Dev's own fresh sentences flagged as machine.

Ms Okafor read the essay again, slowly, next to the 2021 journal. When she looked up, she took off her glasses. “I should have done this on day one,” she said. “Read them side by side. It's the same boy on every page.”

How the school should use the tool

Mr Bryce asked the fair question: “So do we bin the software?” And this was the part Dev was proudest of, because their answer was no. They proposed running the detector the way a well-designed agent loop runs (sense, think, act, with a human where it matters):

🔄 The loop: flag → human reads → talk to the student Sense: the detector screens essays and raises flags; that's all a flag is, a sensor reading.
Think: a named teacher reads the flagged work against the student's past writing, the judgement step machines don't get to do.
Act: a conversation with the student (drafts, questions, context) before any decision.
The tool call (the detector) belongs in the sense step. The moment it was being used as the act step (score straight to accusation), the loop was broken by design.
Checkpoint 5 · Design the policy

The panel asks: “What should our policy actually say?” Which design is right?

The school adopted it in a week, one line at the top of the policy, in bold: “No consequence without a human who has read the work.” Tariq's flag was withdrawn, with a written apology. Ms Halloran got the false-positive maths added to the Year 9 scheme of work, which she called “the only good thing a 92 ever did.”

Assembly

Two weeks later, Ms Okafor asked Tariq to read the essay at year assembly. He said no. Then he thought about it for a day, and said yes: “but only the real one. No edits.”

He stood at the front with a single sheet of paper, and read about his mother in the plainest words in the world. How she checked his journal every night without ever reading it unless invited. How she said fancy sentences were for hiding, and plain ones were for meaning. How the last thing she taught him wasn't a sentence at all.

Tariq · at the microphone “My mother said plain words are not empty words. A machine read my plain words and decided no person was in them. It was wrong. She is in every one of them. She is the reason they are plain.”

The hall was silent: the proper kind, the kind you can stand in the middle of and not drown. Then it wasn't silent at all.

From the back row, Dev grinned and clapped until his hands hurt. Because that was the thing no detector would ever have a token for: he hadn't defended an essay. He'd defended the person his friend's mother had made, and a number, however confident, was never going to be allowed to redefine his friend.

What Dev and Tariq learned

  • Sense → think → act: a detector is a sensor, not a judge; tool calls belong in the sensing step, humans in the judgement step.
  • Confident ≠ correct: models optimise for a signal, not truth; a bold score can be plausibly, fluently wrong.
  • Safeguards are design choices: human oversight, an appeal route and reading the actual work aren't extras; they're the system working as intended.
  • What friendship is for: refusing to let a number redefine someone you know, and learning enough about the number to prove it.
🏅

The End

A score is not a verdict. A friend is not a probability. And plain words are not empty words.
